Dear Fabian,

Thank you for the recommendations. I took them seriously, and I also took you up on the offer of the tool. This is what came out.

On your comments about the manuscript: I have switched to the standard vocabulary — source and target rather than receiver and predecessor — and restructured the work as a conventional paper. I have also tried to write it as a buildings paper rather than a transfer-learning paper: the outcomes are degree-hours and kilowatt-hours, not an abstract transfer score.

The manuscript was prepared with AI assistance, as the disclosure states. The questions, experimental protocol, decisions about what to measure and report, and the interpretation are mine. I would rather be responsible for an imperfect sentence than hide that distinction behind polished prose.

I also followed your more important suggestion: evaluate the transfer on the downstream control task. There is no prediction-error outcome in this paper. The question is simply what the building spent and how cold it became.

And I used BuilDa.

I did not use the ThermBuild dataset — I was unable to fetch the 13 GB simulated set, and for this experiment the generator turned out to be the more useful part anyway. I used BuilDa as the reference for a fast reduced thermal model: eight buildings in Munich to fit two correction factors, followed by four held-out buildings in Berlin in a different month. The corrected model agrees with BuilDa to about 2% on heat demand and within 0.23 K on mean indoor temperature. It can then run a heating season for 300 buildings in about a second, which made the population experiment practical.

I added one element that is not represented in BuilDa in this form. In my bench, available heat-pump capacity falls with outdoor temperature as well as COP. Combined with power sized from the DIN rule used in the BuilDa converter layer, this means that during the coldest window of its own weather record a building can already supply only about 88–95% of its required heat before the imposed failure begins.

The experiment then asks a source-selection question, but with a different inherited object from GenTL: a control policy rather than a predictive model.

I used 40 sources and 200 targets across measured weather from 19 locations. Each target experiences a 72-hour reduction of heating capacity during the coldest window of its own year. I tested whether the value of transferring a source policy could be predicted in two ways: similarity in ten building parameters, and similarity in a functional resilience trait — indoor temperature after 72 hours with heating off at −10 °C.

Across all 8,000 ordered source-target pairs, the correlation between transfer gain and distance in the ten building parameters is +0.013 (p = 0.25). For difference in the resilience trait it is −0.021 (p = 0.06). Neither provides a useful similarity signal, and choosing the nearest source by either measure is worse than choosing a source at random.

The part I did not expect came from looking at the same outcome matrix in the other direction.

Across targets, the mean correlation between sources is +0.94. A source that performs well for one target tends to perform well for almost all of them. Only four of the forty sources were ever best for any target, and two of those were best for 187 of the 200 targets used in this comparison.

So, in this population, source suitability is dominated by source quality rather than source-target matching. For the two similarity measures I tested, there appears to be no useful matching signal to discover.

I do not read this as contradicting the source-selection results you pointed me to. A similarity effect visible with a small target set under one climate may have a different reach when the population is widened. What seems useful to me is that the between-source concordance can be measured before attempting a similarity-based selection study. If it is already very high, the selection problem may have a different shape.

I then tried the obvious alternative: select a source by testing it on a model population rather than by describing its similarity to the target. In 200 random half/half splits of the targets, the source selected on one half achieved 18.9 degree-hours on the unseen half — the same to one decimal place as the best source available on that half.

And then came the result that reminded me most strongly of GenTL.

I had been treating the pooled experience as an aggregation of what individual sources had learned for themselves — essentially a median over their policies. That was the wrong operation in this experiment. Under the same search budget, a single policy searched directly against the model population reached 12.5 degree-hours, compared with 15.4 for the median source policy. It was better than that median for 98% of unseen targets and better than the best individual source policy for 82%.

So my aggregation lost. What replaced it has the same structural form as GenTL, although the inherited object and the downstream measure are different: one solution learned against a population rather than an aggregation of solutions learned by individuals.

There is also a cost, and I have tried not to hide it. Against a target that tuned itself, the inherited median policy uses 277 kWh more over the season and avoids 102 degree-hours of discomfort — about EUR 83 at EUR 0.30/kWh, or EUR 0.81 per avoided degree-hour. Much of the benefit is concentrated in the short failure period, while the reserve is being paid for throughout the winter. That suggests a different next question: whether the reserve can be activated from a leading indicator rather than held continuously. I have not tested that yet.

The attached paper is deliberately short. It also reports two defects I found in my own procedure rather than correcting them silently: an earlier comfort tolerance could be exploited by the search, and the direct population search omitted an energy price from its objective, causing its setpoint to run to the upper bound. The ranking result remains, but I do not present that policy's parameter values as a recommendation.

I have two questions.

First: does this very high between-source concordance look to you like a peculiarity of my population, or is it something you would expect might also appear across the BuilDa source population? If it does, it seems worth measuring before trying to predict source-target compatibility.

Second: is the failure case interesting to you as an experimental direction? What interests me is whether experience acquired under disturbances can itself become transferable population knowledge — not only a better model of the building, but knowledge of what to do when normal operating conditions fail.

I have a longer companion manuscript from which this experiment grew, covering other inherited objects and other technical systems as well. I can send it if it would be useful.

Thank you again for pointing me toward BuilDa. It changed the experiment substantially.

With respect,
