**Subject:** Lamarckian inheritance outside robotics: a draft, and one question

Dear Professor Eiben,

I read your paper with Luo, Miras and Tomczak on Lamarckian principles in robot evolution, and it sent me somewhere you probably did not intend.

In your setting the inherited object is decided in advance. There is a genotype, and lifetime learning is written back into it. That works because the mapping is reversible. I work on buildings, machines and factories, and there the mapping does not exist. A building has no genotype. So before asking how to inherit, I had to ask what — which part of an object's operating history is worth keeping so that another object can use it.

I could not find that measured anywhere, so I built a stand to measure it. The draft is attached. One protocol, three domains: a synthetic population of buildings, hourly records of 40 real non-residential buildings, and six benchmark water networks. An object lives once, no replay, and pays its losses as they come.

Most of the results are negative. A single predecessor's representation, used whole, is usually worse than nothing. Similarity of bodies predicts nothing. And the one closest to your work: the quality a predecessor achieved does not pass to its successors — in two populations the slope was −2.12 and −0.38, negative but undetermined in size. A fund over many predecessors helps 97–98 %; one predecessor helps about half, however good it is. What is inherited is not what a parent acquired but what many have survived.

I also report three defects I found in my own instrument after the experiments were finished.

I am an independent researcher with no group to argue with. That is why I am writing.

If you have twenty minutes, I would value your view on one thing above all: whether the protocol measures what I claim it measures, or whether I have fooled myself somewhere.
